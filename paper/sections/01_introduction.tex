\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Toeplitz matrices are among the simplest matrix models of translation invariance. Their entries are constant along each diagonal, so a matrix of order $N$ is described by only $2N-1$ parameters.
A real Toeplitz matrix has constant entries along each diagonal. For
$x=(x_{-(N-1)},\ldots,x_0,\ldots,x_{N-1})\in\R^{2N-1}$, write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:toeplitz}
 X:=(x_{i-j})_{i,j=1}^N=
 \begin{pmatrix}
  x_0 & x_{-1} & \cdots & x_{-(N-1)}\\
  x_1 & x_0 & \ddots & \vdots\\
  \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & x_{-1}\\
  x_{N-1} & \cdots & x_1 & x_0
 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
They arise naturally in finite convolution, stationary covariance models, and the discretization of translation-invariant operators,
random matrix theory, and structured optimization
\cite{gray2006,yelim2016,bryc2006}.

Let $X$ and $Y$ be Toeplitz matrices as in \eqref{eq:toeplitz}. It is well
known that the B\"ottcher--Wenzel form (BW form) is a nonnegative homogeneous quartic polynomial with the $4N-2$ real variables
$(x_{-(N-1)},\ldots,x_{N-1},y_{-(N-1)},\ldots,y_{N-1})$
\cite{bottcher2008,laszlo2012},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:FN}
\tag{BW form}
 F_N(x,y):=2\norm X_F^2\norm Y_F^2
          -2\langle X,Y\rangle_F^2-\norm{[X,Y]}_F^2
\end{equation}
where
$\langle X,Y\rangle_F=\tr(X^\top Y)$,
$\norm X_F^2=\langle X,X\rangle_F$ and $[X,Y]=XY-YX$\footnote{Indeed, for $X\ne0$, apply the B\"ottcher--Wenzel inequality
$
                  \norm{[U,V]}_F^2\le2\norm U_F^2\norm V_F^2
$
to $U=X$ and $V=Y-\langle X,Y\rangle_FX/\norm X_F^2$;
then $[U,V]=[X,Y]$ and
$\norm X_F^2\norm V_F^2=\norm X_F^2\norm Y_F^2-\langle X,Y\rangle_F^2$,
which gives $F_N(x,y)\ge0$. The case $X=0$ is immediate.}.

A more interesting question is whether this nonnegativity admits a
sum-of-squares (SOS) representation
$F_N(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}q_j(x,y)^2$,
where $r$ is finite and each $q_j$ is a real homogeneous quadratic
polynomial.
Hilbert's classical work established that nonnegative quartic forms
need not be sums of squares in general
\cite{hilbert1888darstellung,choi1995}.
However, the homogeneous quartic in \eqref{eq:FN} is far from arbitrary:
it has strong symmetries, arises from a matrix commutator, and is
nonnegative at every order.
\begin{quote}
\textit{L\'aszl\'o conjectured in 2012 that such a sum-of-squares
(SoS) representation exists for every $N\ge2$
\cite[Conjecture~15]{laszlo2012}.}
\end{quote}

In 2012, L\'aszl\'o established SoS representability for $N\le7$
by expressing the form in wedge coordinates and adjusting its Gram
matrix using Pl\"ucker identities \cite[Section~5]{laszlo2012}.

The conjecture was subsequently discussed by Lu and Wenzel in 2017
\cite{lu2017} and by Ge, Li, Tang and Zhou in their 2024 survey
\cite{ge2024published}; it is also listed as MI-15 in the
OpenProblemsInNLA catalogue \cite{catalogue2026}.
More recently, Holden supplied rational Gram certificates for
$N=8,\ldots,12$, extending the conjectured positive range to $N=12$ \cite{holden2026}.
Also, a related positive result holds for cyclic Hankel matrices, whose
entries depend on $i+j$ modulo $N$: L\'aszl\'o proved that their
B\"ottcher--Wenzel form is SoS at every order
\cite[Theorem~7]{laszlo2012}.
For general Toeplitz pairs, however,  L\'aszl\'o's conjecture for the Toeplitz B\"ottcher--Wenzel form
 has remained open despite substantial positive evidence.
The central structural question, in the spirit of Hilbert's work,
therefore remains:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Is Toeplitz structure sufficient to close the gap between
nonnegativity and sums of squares for this family of quartic forms?}
\end{quote}

In this paper, we answer L\'aszl\'o's conjecture in the negative:
{for every sufficiently large $N$, the Toeplitz
B\"ottcher--Wenzel form $F_N$ is not SoS. }The main contribution of the
paper is threefold.

\paragraph{The Toeplitz B\"ottcher--Wenzel form is not always a sum of squares.}
\rev{The proof isolates an explicit corner-supported subclass of Toeplitz
pairs and reduces all positive semidefinite Gram representations to a
common normal form.  A finite complex test then separates a fixed
Gram-invariant contribution from the remaining Gram-dependent part.
The former has a strictly negative limiting direction, while the latter
vanishes uniformly over all positive semidefinite Gram representations.
This contradiction proves that the Toeplitz B\"ottcher--Wenzel form is
not SoS for all sufficiently large orders.  At the explicit sufficient
threshold, the negative theorem is also formally verified in Lean~4;
see Appendix~\ref{app:lean-negative}.}

\paragraph{Symmetry subclasses remain SoS at every order.}
Although L\'aszl\'o's conjecture is false in general, we establish
a broad positive result valid for all $N$:
\begin{center}
\emph{If either $X$ or $Y$ is symmetric or skew-symmetric,
then the corresponding restriction of $F_N$ is SoS at every order,
with the other factor allowed to be any real Toeplitz matrix.}
\end{center}
To the best of our knowledge, these explicit one-sided Toeplitz
SoS representations have not previously appeared in the literature. These subclasses include several familiar matrix models.
For instance, covariance matrices of real weakly stationary processes
are symmetric Toeplitz \cite{gray2006}, whereas odd convolution filters
and centered first-difference stencils give skew-symmetric Toeplitz
matrices. In the finite Fourier symbol
$f_x(\theta)=\sum_{k=-(N-1)}^{N-1}x_ke^{ik\theta}$,
the symmetric and skew-symmetric conditions correspond, respectively,
to cosine terms and purely imaginary sine terms.
Our result therefore provides explicit SoS representations for the
B\"ottcher--Wenzel form on these subclasses at every order.

\paragraph{The Toeplitz B\"ottcher--Wenzel form remains SoS at small orders.}
We establish SoS representability for every $2\le N\le20$, with
proofs checked in Lean~4, extending the previously certified range
$2\le N\le12$. We further extend the positive range to $2\le N\le50$
through an exact chain of rational identities, in which each form
is obtained from its predecessor by a positive scaling, a linear
substitution, and the addition of an SoS polynomial.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:non-sos}, Section~\ref{sec:positive-subclasses}, and
Section~\ref{sec:exact-certificates} establish the three contributions
described above, respectively.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper and discusses the
remaining open questions.
The appendices collect the supporting technical material, including the
complete wedge-Gram characterization, the exact negative witness,
the explicit large-order bound, the detailed positive identities, and
the verification procedures and code instructions for the finite-order
results.
